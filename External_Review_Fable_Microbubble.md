## Executive verdict

1. **An air-driven submerged Venturi is not the inverse of a water-driven nanobubble generator.** At 0.2–0.6 bar(g), air can produce a high-velocity gas jet and substantial entrainment, but it does not provide the dense liquid motive stream normally responsible for intense liquid shear, recirculation, pressure reduction, and controlled bubble breakup.

2. **A porous diffuser pore size is not the final bubble size.** A 5–10 µm pore can easily detach bubbles hundreds of micrometres in diameter in quiescent water.

3. **A Roots blower alone is not a credible route to a controllable, stable bulk nanobubble generator below 200 nm.** It may produce a small nanoparticle-sized population under special conditions, but it cannot honestly be specified as a nanobubble system without rigorous particle-size, gas-content, and lifetime measurements.

4. **The realistic product opportunity is a high-flux microbubble retrofit**, probably producing a broad distribution centred somewhere between approximately 50 and 300 µm under optimized hydrodynamic conditions—not guaranteed 20–80 µm bubbles directly from static pores.

# 1. Air-driven underwater Venturi

## 1.1 What occurs in a conventional water-driven nanobubble generator?

A commercial liquid-driven generator generally has:

- a pressurized liquid stream;
- a deliberately designed contraction/throat;
- gas injection into that liquid stream;
- a pressure-recovery or breakup section;
- often recirculation of a substantial liquid flow.

The water is the continuous phase. Its density is approximately:

$$\rho_w \approx 1{,}000\text{–}1{,}025\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$$

whereas air at pond conditions is approximately:

$$\rho_a \approx 1.2\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}$$

The density ratio is therefore roughly:

$$\frac{\rho_w}{\rho_a}\approx 800$$

At equal velocity, water carries approximately 800 times more momentum per unit volume than air.

That difference is fundamental. A water-driven ejector uses the liquid stream to:

- entrain gas;
- stretch and deform gas structures;
- generate strong liquid-side turbulence;
- create rapid pressure changes;
- repeatedly break and reform gas interfaces.

## 1.2 What happens when air is the motive fluid?

A submerged air nozzle supplied with 0.3–0.5 bar(g) behaves primarily as a **gas injector or submerged jet**, not as the conventional liquid-motive Venturi used in nanobubble equipment.

The gas may:

- accelerate strongly through a nozzle;
- form a turbulent air plume;
- entrain surrounding water;
- generate large bubbles that fragment partially;
- produce some smaller bubbles through wake instability and turbulent breakup.

However, several limitations appear.

### A. The gas jet has low momentum density

The gas can have high velocity, but velocity alone is not sufficient. Turbulent stresses available to the surrounding water depend on momentum and energy flux. The air has low density, and much of the blower pressure is consumed by:

- hydrostatic head;
- diffuser or nozzle pressure loss;
- gas acceleration;
- two-phase expansion;
- line and fitting losses.

The gas jet can be energetic locally, but the liquid-side shear is generally much less controlled than in a pressurized liquid loop.

### B. The gas is compressible

A liquid Venturi has relatively predictable pressure and velocity fields. An air jet is compressible and may approach choked-flow conditions in a small restriction.

At 0.4 bar(g), the absolute upstream pressure is approximately 1.4 bar(a). Expansion into water at roughly 1.1–1.2 bar(a), depending on depth, is significant. The jet therefore expands rapidly and loses the compact, high-shear liquid-throat structure that makes a conventional Venturi effective.

### C. It does not create ordinary hydrodynamic cavitation in the same way

Hydrodynamic cavitation is primarily a liquid phenomenon. The liquid pressure must fall below its vapour-pressure threshold, allowing vapour cavities to form and subsequently collapse.

An air jet injected into water does not automatically create this condition. It creates a gas phase already present in the liquid, not necessarily vapour cavities generated by liquid pressure falling below vapour pressure.

Gas injection can produce:

- air cavities;
- gas-filled recirculation zones;
- violent interface oscillations;
- occasional bubble breakup.

Those are not equivalent to controlled hydrodynamic cavitation with repeated high-pressure bubble collapse.

### D. Cavitation inception is not likely at this pressure

A common dimensionless cavitation index is:

$$\sigma=\frac{p_\infty-p_v}{\tfrac12\rho_w U^2}$$

where:

- $p_\infty$ is the local liquid pressure;
- $p_v$ is water vapour pressure;
- $U$ is the characteristic liquid velocity.

At 1.2–1.8 m depth, the ambient liquid pressure is already approximately 12–18 kPa above atmospheric pressure. To obtain strong liquid cavitation, the local liquid pressure must be reduced substantially below the surrounding pressure.

An air jet may create local low-pressure regions, but it does not reliably provide:

- a liquid-dominated throat;
- a sufficiently low liquid pressure;
- repeatable cavitation inception;
- a controlled collapse zone.

Therefore, “air through an underwater Venturi” should not be marketed as a hydrodynamic nanobubble generator unless direct measurements prove otherwise.

## 1.3 Can the air jet create microbubbles?

Yes, potentially.

An air nozzle, air-lift geometry, fluidic oscillator, or multi-orifice injector can produce bubble breakup through:

- turbulent shear;
- jet instability;
- wake interaction;
- oscillatory forcing;
- impingement;
- collision with a perforated breakup element.

But producing occasional bubbles below 100 µm is not the same as producing a dense, stable, energy-efficient microbubble population with a narrow 20–80 µm distribution.

# 2. Pore physics and bubble detachment

## 2.1 Bubble-point pressure

Using the equation specified:

$$\Delta P=\frac{4\gamma\cos\theta}{d_\text{pore}}$$

and taking:

$$\gamma=0.072\ \mathrm{N/m}$$

with $\cos\theta=1$ for the maximum-wetting idealization:

$$\Delta P=\frac{0.288}{d_\text{pore}}$$

where $d_\text{pore}$ is in metres.

| Pore diameter | Ideal capillary pressure | Approximate pressure |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 10 µm | $28.8\ \mathrm{kPa}$ | $0.288\ \mathrm{bar}$ |
| 1 µm | $288\ \mathrm{kPa}$ | $2.88\ \mathrm{bar}$ |
| 100 nm | $2.88\ \mathrm{MPa}$ | $28.8\ \mathrm{bar}$ |

For an underwater diffuser, the air pressure must exceed approximately:

$$P_\text{required} = P_\text{hydrostatic} + P_\text{capillary} + P_\text{diffuser loss} + P_\text{line loss}$$

At 1.2–1.8 m water depth, the hydrostatic pressure alone is approximately $0.12\text{–}0.18\ \mathrm{bar}$.
Therefore, an ideal 10 µm pore at 1.8 m depth already requires roughly:
$$0.288+0.18=0.468\ \mathrm{bar}$$
before accounting for membrane loss or fouling.

### Consequence

- **10 µm pores:** possibly operable near the upper end of a 0.6 bar blower system, but with limited flow margin.
- **1 µm pores:** not realistically operable from a 0.2–0.6 bar Roots blower.
- **100 nm pores:** completely outside the pressure capability of the proposed system.

## 2.2 Why a 10 µm pore does not make a 10 µm bubble

Once air emerges from a pore, the bubble grows outside the pore until the upward buoyancy force exceeds the capillary retention force:

$$d_b \approx \left( \frac{6d_p\gamma\cos\theta}{\rho_w g} \right)^{1/3}$$

For seawater-like density and $\cos\theta\approx1$:
- **10 µm pore:** $d_b\approx 0.76\ \mathrm{mm}$ (760 µm)
- **5 µm pore:** $d_b\approx 0.60\ \mathrm{mm}$ (600 µm)

The bubble must grow to hundreds of micrometres before its buoyancy becomes large enough to detach.

## 2.3 What makes bubbles smaller?

To obtain bubbles in the 20–80 µm range, the system needs to detach or fragment the bubble before normal buoyancy-driven growth. Required mechanisms include:

- high cross-flow over the pore surface;
- liquid-side turbulent shear;
- very low gas flow per pore;
- oscillatory forcing;
- rapidly alternating pressure;
- co-flowing liquid;
- an external breakup zone;
- moving or vibrating interfaces;
- impingement or collision.

# 3. Can low-pressure air alone generate stable bulk nanobubbles?

## Strict physics answer

**Not reliably in the proposed configuration.**

Under these conditions (no liquid pump, no pressurized water recirculation, no ultrasonic field, no high-speed rotor, only 0.2–0.6 bar air, dirty pond water), a device should not be expected to generate a repeatable, dense population of stable bulk nanobubbles below 200 nm.

> **A Roots blower may generate fine bubbles and perhaps a minor submicrometre population, but it is not a credible stand-alone nanobubble platform without an additional liquid-side breakup mechanism and independent verification.**

---

# 4. What is realistically achievable with Roots blowers?

## 4.1 The realistic target

- **primary bubble population:** approximately 50–300 µm;
- **aspirational target:** 20–80 µm under controlled test conditions;
- **actual pond distribution:** likely broader, with some 0.1–1 mm bubbles;
- **nanobubble fraction:** unclaimed unless independently measured.

## 4.2 Fluidic oscillators

Roots blower air enters a fluidic oscillator manifold $\rightarrow$ creates alternating or pulsating jets $\rightarrow$ discharges through small submerged nozzles $\rightarrow$ impinge on a nearby liquid-shear or perforated breakup zone $\rightarrow$ release dense microbubble cloud.

## 4.3 Biofouling in shrimp ponds

Fouling sources: bacterial biofilm, algae, suspended clay/silt, feed fines, shrimp faeces.
Need: removable diffuser cassettes, coarse upstream air filtration, back-purge capability, quick-release cleaning.

# 5. Recommended commercial architecture

## Final recommendation

Change the specification from:
> “air-only nanobubble generator”

to:
> **“air-powered hydrodynamic microbubble diffuser and pond-mixing retrofit, optimized for 20–60 kPa Roots-blower systems.”**
